# ADR-0010: role-based access control, and the line it does not cross

**Status:** accepted — supersedes part of [ADR-0009](0009-invite-only.md)

## Context

The first design had exactly one administrative identity: a token in the environment.
That is fine while the operator is the only person who ever administers anything, and it
stops being fine the moment a second person needs to help — with onboarding, with somebody
locked out, with cleaning up after somebody who left. The alternatives were to share the
environment token (which is unattributable and unrevokable without rotating it for
everyone) or to build this.

## Decision

Real RBAC. Permissions are a closed enum; roles are named sets of them; roles are data, so
a deployment can define whatever it needs. Accounts hold roles, and an account's
permissions are the union of its roles', resolved fresh on every request.

Four built-in roles — `owner`, `admin`, `auditor`, `member` — are immutable so that
`member` always means what it says. `member` is what every new account gets.

### The line this does not cross

**No permission grants access to another account's credentials.** There is no
`credentials:read_any`, and `profiles:read_any` returns metadata only: which services
somebody has connected and whether each still works, never what is behind them.

This is the most important sentence in the ADR. The operator can already decrypt the
vault — they hold the master key ([ADR-0006](0006-server-can-decrypt.md)) — so it might
look as though such a permission would concede nothing new. It would concede a great deal.
Decrypting with the master key requires shell access on the box, leaves evidence, and
cannot be handed to somebody else. A *permission* that produced another person's token
would be remote, silent, usable from a browser, and grantable to anyone. Those are
different threats with the same name.

So administration here means managing accounts, not becoming them. `profiles:delete_any`
exists — cleaning up after somebody who has left is a real need — and it destroys without
reading, which is exactly the asymmetry that makes it safe to grant.

## The guards, and what each one closes

**You cannot grant a permission you do not hold.** Applied to assigning a role *and* to
creating or editing one. Either alone is bypassed by doing the other first: write a role
containing the permission you want, then grant it to yourself. Both together close it.

**You cannot act on an account more privileged than you.** The granting rule alone is not
enough, and this was found by a security review rather than by design. Resetting somebody's
password is close to *becoming* them — whoever holds the token sets the password — so an
`admin` who could not grant themselves `roles:write` could simply reset the **owner's**
password and log in as somebody who already had it. With mail disabled, which is the
default, the plaintext token even comes back in the response body. Every guard on the
granting path was decoration until this landed.

The same reasoning covers disabling, signing out and deleting: each is an action against a
person, and being able to take it against somebody more privileged is a way to remove the
people who could stop you. Acting on yourself is always allowed, so it never blocks
somebody managing their own account, and peers can act on peers — the rule is a subset
check, not a hierarchy, which is what lets two administrators cover for each other.

**Permission is checked before existence.** Every administrative method checks the
permission first and looks the target up second, so a caller without the permission gets
403 for an account that exists and 403 for one that does not. The other order turns the
403/404 difference into an account-id oracle for anyone with a login.

**Built-in roles are immutable.** Editing `member` into something powerful would be the
quietest privilege escalation available: everybody's permissions change and nobody's roles
do.

**A role in use cannot be deleted.** Refused rather than cascaded, because silently
stripping a permission from everyone who had it is discovered when somebody cannot do
their job.

**The last owner cannot be demoted or deleted** — and the check happens *inside the store's
lock*, together with the write. Performed by a caller instead, two administrators demoting
the two remaining owners at the same moment would both read "there are two" and both
proceed. The window is small and the consequence is permanent: appointing an owner
requires being one.

**Permissions are never cached.** Not on the session, not in a token. Revoking a role takes
effect on that account's very next request, rather than whenever they next log in.

## Bootstrapping, and getting back in

**The first account ever created becomes the owner.** Somebody has to be able to appoint
owners and appointing one requires already being one, so without this a fresh deployment
could only ever be administered through the environment token.

**`KEYRING_ADMIN_TOKEN` survives as break-glass.** It authorises any administrative request,
holds every permission, is exempt from the subset rule (it has no permission set of its own
to exceed), and is recorded in the audit log as `break-glass` rather than as an account.
That conspicuousness is deliberate: an audit log full of break-glass entries means somebody
is using the recovery path as a convenience.

It exists because the last-owner guard is a safety net, and safety nets need a way down.
If it is unset, break-glass is refused — never waved through, because an administrative
route that falls back to "no token required" is how a service ships with an
unauthenticated account factory.

## The audit log

RBAC without an audit trail is a set of permissions nobody can be held to. Every action one
account takes *on another* is recorded: actor, action, target, time. Actions on your own
account are not, because recording them would bury the entries that matter.

Entries carry opaque account ids rather than addresses, and never a secret. An audit log is
read by more people than the vault is, and kept for longer.

## What it costs

A meaningful amount of surface area — a permission enum, a role store, an admin service, a
router, and the largest single body of tests in the project. Most of those tests are
written as an attacker, because an account holding an administrative role is now the most
valuable thing in this service to steal.

It also means the environment token is no longer the only thing that can administer the
service, which is the point and is also a larger attack surface than one token was.

## What would change our minds

Nothing at this scale. If the deployment ever grew past people the operator knows, the next
step is not more permissions but an external identity provider behind the same
`Authenticator` port — see [ADR-0008](0008-opaque-sessions-signed-service-tokens.md).
